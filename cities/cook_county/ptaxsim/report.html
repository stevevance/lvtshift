What if vacant land were assessed like commercial property?

Cook County assesses class 1-00 vacant land at 10% of market value — the residential rate. Commercial property is assessed at 25%. This models one change and nothing else: 1-00 moves to 25%, and every other class, every taxing body's levy, the equalizer, and every exemption stay exactly as they are.

This is not a land value tax. There is no split rate, no building abatement, and no shift of tax onto land. It is a reassessment of one class inside the existing classified system — which is why the effect on everyone else is measured in fractions of a percent rather than in double digits.

Why class 8 falls three times as far

Every class outside vacant land lands within a hair of −0.9% — except class 8, the commercial and industrial incentive class for the south suburbs, which falls −3.11%. Nothing special is being done to class 8. It sits in the tax codes that hold a far larger share of the county's vacant land, so when that land is reassessed, those districts' bases grow proportionally more and their rates fall proportionally further.

The same geography works the other way for the parcels that gain most: relief concentrates wherever vacant land is thickest, which in Cook County means the south and west suburbs rather than the Loop.

How the bills were rebuilt

Every figure here comes from the Cook County Assessor's own ptaxsim database and the bill algorithm published with it. No part of this analysis shares code or parameters with the LVTShift model of the same county — it is an independent second opinion.

All 1,859,067 tax-year-2024 parcels are rebuilt from Clerk-stage assessed value, the twelve exemption columns, and each taxing agency's extension over its EAV base:

eav         = round(av_clerk × 3.0355)          (class 239 farmland: eav = av)
exe_total   = sum of the twelve exe_* columns
taxable_eav = max(eav − exe_total, 0), and 0 when eav − exe_total < 150
agency_rate = agency.total_ext ÷ agency.cty_cook_eav
bill        = taxable_eav × Σ agency_rate over the pin's tax code

What this model does not do

  • The TIF share of each parcel is held at its 2024 value. A real reassessment would move a TIF's base-versus-increment split; the effect is second-order and confined to that $13.8M line.
  • The transit-TIF carve-out, where Chicago Public Schools takes its full share ahead of the TIF, is not modeled. It redistributes among districts and changes no taxpayer's bill.
  • Per-agency rounding to the cent, which real bills apply, is not reproduced. It is worth well under five cents a bill and cancels in aggregate.
  • No behavioral response. Owners who find land more expensive to hold may build on it or sell it, which is the entire point of the policy — and the reason these first-round numbers are a ceiling on the burden and a floor on the benefit.
